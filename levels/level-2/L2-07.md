## What you will learn

- I can draw a chain, a fork, and a collider, and name each one.
- I can explain why picking a group by a collider can make two unrelated causes look linked.
- I can say why a causal graph is a guess to be tested, not a fact read from data.

## Story: Three shapes, one surprise

Leo is late for school on a rainy morning. Rain makes the road wet, and a wet road slows the traffic. "That is a chain," says Maya: rain -&gt; wet road -&gt; slow traffic. In this simple map, if you already know the road is wet, hearing that it rained tells you nothing new about the traffic.

Next, Maya points at a news chart. In summer, ice cream sales rise, and so do sunburns. Neither one causes the other. Summer is a common cause, so the map is a fork: ice cream &lt;- summer -&gt; sunburn. If you compare only July days, most of the link between ice cream and sunburn fades away.

Then their friend Priya talks about an imagined arts school that is hard to get into. Maya invents a test with made-up numbers. There are 150 made-up applicants, each with a talent score and a hard work score from 1 to 10. The two scores are random and unrelated. The school admits a student only if talent plus hard work is above 13. That rule lets in 46 admitted students.

Among those 46 admitted students, a pattern appears. The ones with less talent tend to have more hard work. Why? A student with low talent only gets in by working very hard. A very talented student can get in with less work. The rule picks students by a collider: talent -&gt; admitted &lt;- hard work.

"So the pattern comes from the choosing, not from the students," says Leo. Maya nods. "And every map we drew today is a guess. Data can support a map or argue against it. The arrows never appear on their own."

## Hands-on: Sort the stories, then play the admissions game

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- 9 index cards or slips of paper
- A coin
- A six-sided die
- A pencil and paper for a table
- A calculator (optional)

**Steps:**

1. Copy one story onto each card. (a) A storm cuts the power, the fridge warms up, and the milk spoils. (b) Good grades or a sports award can each win a prize.
2. (c) Hot weather raises fan sales and also raises pool visits. (d) In children, age affects both shoe size and reading level. (e) Missing the bus makes you late, and being late means missing the warm-up.
3. (f) A dead battery or a broken bulb can each make a flashlight stay dark. (g) Great food or a great view can each make a cafe busy. (h) Practice builds skill, and skill wins games. (i) A festival fills the hotels and jams the roads.
4. Sort the cards into three piles: chain, fork, and collider. Draw each story as boxes and arrows.
5. Now the admissions game. Flip the coin for talent: heads scores 3 and tails scores 0. Roll the die for hard work, from 1 to 6.
6. Add the two scores. A total of 6 or more means the student is admitted. Play 30 students and record talent, hard work, and admitted or not.
7. For all 30 students, find the average die roll for heads and for tails. Then do the same using only the admitted students.
8. Compare the two pairs of averages. Among the admitted, which talent group needed more hard work?

:::{tip} Record it
Your three drawn graphs, your 30-row table, and your four averages. Card key: chains a, e, h; forks c, d, i; colliders b, f, g.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Roll the die and flip the coin on a table so nothing rolls onto the floor.
:::

## Practice together

In the admissions game, work out exactly which rolls get a student admitted. What is the average hard work score of admitted students for tails and for heads?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Tails gives 0, so the student needs a 6 on the die. Heads gives 3, so the student needs 3, 4, 5, or 6. Among admitted students, tails students average 6 and heads students average 4.5. Among everyone, both groups average 3.5, because the coin and die are unrelated. The admission rule made the link.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** Exercise improves fitness, and fitness lowers resting heart rate. Which shape is this? In this map, if you compare only people with the same fitness, does exercise tell you more about heart rate?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
It is a chain: exercise -&gt; fitness -&gt; heart rate. In this simple map, once you know fitness, exercise adds no new information about heart rate. Real bodies may have other paths, so the map is a guess to test.
:::

**2.** A website lists only cafes with at least 4 stars. Stars go up with tasty food and with low prices. Among the listed cafes, cheap ones seem to have worse food. What could explain this?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Stars are a collider: tasty food -&gt; stars &lt;- low prices. A cheap cafe can reach 4 stars with ordinary food, but an expensive one needs great food. Listing only 4-star cafes can create the pattern even if price and taste are unrelated.
:::

## Talk about it

- Why does looking only at admitted students change what you see, even though no student changed?
- Think of a team, a club, or a contest that picks people by two different strengths. What fake pattern might appear among the people picked?
- If two people draw different graphs for the same story, how could data help you decide between them?

## Check yourself

**1. Which map is a fork?**

- **A.** talent -&gt; admitted &lt;- hard work
- **B.** rain -&gt; wet road -&gt; slow traffic
- **C.** ice cream &lt;- summer -&gt; sunburn

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. ice cream &lt;- summer -&gt; sunburn**

In a fork, one cause points to two effects. Summer affects both ice cream sales and sunburns.
:::

**2. In the made-up admissions data, among the 46 admitted students, less talent went with more hard work. In all 150 applicants, the two were unrelated. What is the best explanation?**

- **A.** Choosing students by talent plus hard work created the pattern.
- **B.** Working hard lowers talent.
- **C.** Talent makes students lazy.

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**A. Choosing students by talent plus hard work created the pattern.**

Admission is a collider. Picking only students above the cutoff means low talent must be balanced by high hard work, so a downward pattern appears.
:::

**3. Remember Lesson 3. In an imagined town, months with more sunscreen sold also have more sunburns. What does the scatter plot alone show?** *(Review of L2-03)*

- **A.** Sunburns cause people to buy sunscreen.
- **B.** Sunscreen sales and sunburns rise together in this data.
- **C.** Sunscreen causes sunburn.

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. Sunscreen sales and sunburns rise together in this data.**

A scatter plot shows an association. Here sunny weather is a likely common cause of both, and the plot cannot tell which way causes run.

## Words to know

**causal graph**
: A drawing of boxes and arrows. Each arrow says we think one thing directly affects another.

**collider**
: A box that two arrows point into, as in talent -&gt; admitted &lt;- hard work.

**condition on**
: To look only at the records in one group, such as only admitted students or only July days.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** The lesson introduces the three building blocks of causal diagrams: chains, forks (common causes) and colliders. Pearl's overview explains how conditioning on the middle of a chain or fork blocks an association, while conditioning on a collider can open one [@pearl]. Bareinboim's causal AI textbook builds on the same graphs [@bareinboim]. Gopnik and colleagues argue that young children learn causal maps of this kind from patterns in data and from their own interventions [@gopnik2004]. The lesson presents graphs as working guesses because data can support or challenge a graph but cannot draw it alone. The collider scatter uses the course's seeded generator, so the 150 applicants and the 46 admitted match the text.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- Looking inside a group always makes the picture clearer. (Conditioning on a collider can create a link that is not there in the whole population.)
- If two things are linked among admitted students, one must cause the other. (The selection rule itself can produce the link.)
- A causal graph is read straight off the data. (Arrows are assumptions; data can support or challenge a graph, but cannot draw it alone.)
- In a fork, the two effects cause each other. (They share a cause; neither needs to affect the other.)

**To make it easier.** Skip the card sort and draw only the three story graphs. Play the admissions game with 12 students and compare only the admitted ones.

**To make it harder.** Pool the class's admissions games, or list all 12 coin and die outcomes and work out the exact averages. Then change the cutoff to 4 or 5 and predict how the pattern changes before checking.

**Limits.** The chain, fork and collider stories are simplified maps. Real situations usually have more causes and more arrows, and the collider data are made up.
