## Story: Which tutoring program works better?

Leo's imagined school tries two tutoring programs, A and B. Each program helps 100 students. All the numbers here are made up. Some students take easy courses and some take hard courses. Leo counts who passed.

In easy courses, program A passed 18 of 20 students, which is 90 percent. Program B passed 64 of 80, which is 80 percent. In hard courses, A passed 32 of 80, which is 40 percent. B passed 6 of 20, which is 30 percent. So A is ahead in easy courses and in hard courses.

Then Maya adds the groups together. Program A passed 18 + 32 = 50 of 100, which is 50 percent. Program B passed 64 + 6 = 70 of 100, which is 70 percent. Now B looks much better! The comparison flipped. This is called a Simpson's reversal.

The reason is the mix of students. Most of A's students, 80 of 100, were in hard courses, where everyone passes less often. Most of B's students were in easy courses. So the combined rate mostly measures which courses the students took. Leo draws a causal map. Course difficulty points to the program and to passing. Difficulty came first and affects both, so if this map is right, the fair test compares A and B within each group. Then A wins. With a different causal story, the combined numbers could be the right ones.

A real case like this was published in 1975 about graduate school admissions at the University of California, Berkeley. Across the whole university, men were admitted at a higher rate than women. Inside most departments, the gap vanished or slightly favored women. Women had applied more often to the departments that admitted a small share of everyone who applied.

## Hands-on: Find the flip, then build your own

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- Paper and a pencil.
- A calculator (optional).
- About 40 counters, coins or beans in two colors.
- Two cups or bowls.

**Steps:**

1. Here is a made-up table about two imagined pizza shops, P and Q. On sunny days, P is on time for 45 of 50 orders and Q for 136 of 160. On rainy days, P is on time for 75 of 150 orders and Q for 16 of 40.
2. Find each shop's on-time rate on sunny days and on rainy days. Which shop is ahead in each kind of weather?
3. Find each shop's combined rate. Add its on-time orders and divide by all its orders. Which shop is ahead now?
4. Explain the flip in one sentence. Look at how many rainy-day orders each shop had.
5. Now build your own reversal with counters. One color means success, the other means failure. Make two groups, easy and hard, for two options, X and Y.
6. Arrange the counters so X beats Y in both groups. Then give X mostly hard cases and Y mostly easy cases.
7. Count the combined rates. If X is not behind yet, move more of X's cases into the hard group and try again.
8. Draw a causal map for your story. Which comparison would you trust, and why?

:::{tip} Record it
Your rates for both shops, your own counter table with group and combined rates, and your causal map with one sentence of reasoning.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Keep small counters, coins and beans away from young children who might swallow them.
:::

## Practice together

Use Leo's made-up numbers. Why can you not just average A's two rates, 90 percent and 40 percent, to get A's combined rate?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
The average of 90 and 40 is 65, but that treats both groups as the same size. A had only 20 students in easy courses and 80 in hard courses. Counting students gives 50 of 100, which is 50 percent. The big hard group pulls A's combined rate down.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** Two imagined players take shots (made-up numbers). On close shots, A scores 8 of 10 and B scores 35 of 50. On far shots, A scores 20 of 50 and B scores 3 of 10. Who is better at each kind of shot? Who is better combined?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Close: A has 80 percent and B has 70 percent. Far: A has 40 percent and B has 30 percent. So A is better at both. Combined, A scores 28 of 60, about 47 percent, and B scores 38 of 60, about 63 percent. A took far more of the hard, far shots.
:::

**2.** In the pizza table, would you choose a shop by the combined rate or by the weather rates? Use a causal map to explain.

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Rain makes every shop late more often, and P got far more of its orders on rainy days. So weather sits behind both the mix of orders and being on time. On any given kind of day, the weather rates are the fair comparison, and P wins both.
:::

- Can you think of another place where a total might hide what happens inside groups?

## Words to know

**Simpson's reversal**
: When a comparison points one way inside every group but the other way once the groups are combined.

**combined rate**
: All the successes from every group divided by all the cases from every group.

**causal map**
: A drawing with arrows from causes to the things they affect. It shows what we assume, not a proven fact.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** The lesson shows Simpson's reversal with made-up numbers chosen so that every rate is a whole percent and both programs have 100 students: easy 18/20 vs 64/80, hard 32/80 vs 6/20, combined 50/100 vs 70/100. Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell analyzed Berkeley graduate admissions and found that the aggregate gap favoring men mostly disappeared within departments, because women applied more to departments with low admission rates [@bickel1975]; the lesson describes this in words only. Pearl's overview explains that whether to adjust for a variable depends on the assumed causal model, which is why the choice between split and combined data cannot be made from the numbers alone [@pearl]. Cunningham's text covers causal comparisons, confounders and research design at a level suitable for further reading by teachers [@seeing]. Here difficulty is a common cause measured before the program, so the split comparison is the fair one under that assumed map.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- The combined numbers are always the true answer. (They mix in differences in who got which option; with a common cause, compare within groups.)
- The split numbers are always the true answer. (If the program itself changes the grouping variable, splitting can hide part of its effect; the causal map decides.)
- A reversal means someone made an arithmetic mistake. (The arithmetic is right; unequal group sizes produce the flip.)
- The Berkeley data proved there was no bias anywhere. (It showed the overall gap was mostly explained by where people applied; it did not settle why women applied where they did.)

**To make it easier.** Work only with Leo's table. Give the four group rates and ask learners to add the counts for the two combined rates, then say which program looks better each way.

**To make it harder.** Find the smallest change to Leo's table that stops the reversal. Then read about the 1975 Berkeley study and explain, without numbers, why department choice acts like course difficulty.

**Limits.** All tutoring, pizza and shooting numbers are invented. The causal map is an assumption; if other causes of passing differ between programs, even the split comparison may not show the program's effect.
