## Learn

A combined comparison can point in the opposite direction from comparisons within groups. This is called a Simpson reversal. Different mixtures of easier and harder cases can change the combined rates even when each within-group comparison has the same direction.

Consider an observational study of training success. If one method is used mostly for harder tasks, its combined success rate can look worse. Compare the counts within task difficulty as well as the totals. Never average group percentages without their correct weights.

The reversal does not tell you automatically which comparison is causal. The answer depends on the question and the causal role of the grouping variable. A cause measured before treatment can require different handling from a variable caused by the treatment.

:::{important} Key relation
:class: qcai-equation
Combined success rate = total successes across groups / total cases across groups.
:::

:::{tip} Worked example
For easy tasks, method A succeeds in 90/100 cases and B in 80/100. For hard tasks, A succeeds in 10/100 and B in 1/20. A has the higher rate in each group. Combined, A gives 100/200=0.5 and B gives 81/120=0.675.
:::

## Practice

:::{admonition} Exercise
:class: tip

For mild cases, A succeeds in 8/10 and B in 35/50. For severe cases, A succeeds in 20/50 and B in 3/10. Compare each group and the combined totals.
:::

:::{dropdown} Solution

A exceeds B within mild cases, 0.8 versus 0.7, and severe cases, 0.4 versus 0.3. Combined A is 28/60, while B is 38/60. Unequal case mixtures reverse the comparison.
:::

## Check your understanding

**What does a Simpson reversal require you to inspect?**

- **A.** Group counts, weights, and causal roles
- **B.** Only the largest percentage
- **C.** Only the method names

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**A. Group counts, weights, and causal roles**

The group counts explain the weighting. A causal interpretation also needs the role of the grouping variable, not merely a preferred numerical comparison.
:::

:::{caution} Scope and assumptions
Stratification is not always valid adjustment. The causal graph must support the chosen comparison.
:::
