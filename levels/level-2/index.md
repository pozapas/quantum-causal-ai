![Level 2 icon](../../_static/icons/qicon-level-2.svg) **Grades 7–9**: Ages 12 to 15 (grades 7 to 9), working mostly on their own

**The big question:** When does a pattern in data tell us what causes what, and how would we check?

Learners write rules with variables and functions, read graphs with care, and calculate probabilities inside a group. They use natural frequencies to reverse a condition, see a comparison flip when groups are combined, and draw causal maps with chains, common causes, and colliders. They learn why setting a value is different from selecting records with that value. Finally they write a two-outcome chance as a vector and compare it with the state vectors used in quantum physics.
:::

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who it is for | Learners aged about 12 to 15 who have finished Level 1 or are comfortable with fractions, simple probability, and reading tables. They can work through a lesson alone. A teacher, parent, or study partner can join the discussion questions and look over the project. |
| Lessons | 10 lessons of about 45 minutes, plus a 3-part final project |
| Pace | Two lessons a week takes about six weeks, including the final project. Each lesson splits well into two sittings: story, animation and activity first, practice and checks second. |
| Total sittings | 13 |

## How a lesson works

1. Warm up: answer the look-back question from memory.
2. Read the story and watch the animation.
3. Do the activity and keep a record.
4. Work through the guided example, then try two on your own.
5. Discuss, then answer the three check questions. Commit to an answer before you open it.
6. Teachers and parents: read the short guide at the end of the page.

## Lessons

| Lesson | Big idea | Hands-on |
|---|---|---|
| [L2-01 · Name quantities with variables](#lesson-l2-01) | A variable is a letter that stands for a quantity that can change, like a number of tickets or a cost in dollars. An equation states a rule that links variables. Some rules are set by people, like a price list. Others only describe a pattern in data, and a pattern is not the same as a law of cause. | Three price plans on one grid |
| [L2-02 · Functions map inputs to outputs](#lesson-l2-02) | A function is a rule that gives exactly one output for each input. When we use a function as a model of the real world, its output is a prediction, not a measurement. The gap between them is called the residual: observed minus predicted. | Function machines and a coin tower |
| [L2-03 · Read plots without inventing causes](#lesson-l2-03) | A scatter plot shows how two quantities vary together. You can describe its direction, form and strength, and spot points that do not fit. The plot alone cannot tell you what causes what, which way a cause runs, or what happens outside the range of the data. | Plot it, then doubt it |
| [L2-04 · Calculate conditional probability](#lesson-l2-04) | A conditional probability counts inside one group. The group you condition on becomes the whole, so its total goes on the bottom of the fraction. If knowing one thing does not change the chance of the other, the two events are independent. | Doubles and big sums |
| [L2-05 · Reverse a condition with Bayes' rule](#lesson-l2-05) | P(positive \| condition) and P(condition \| positive) are different numbers. To turn one into the other, count the people in an imagined group. When a condition is rare, most positive tests can be false alarms, even from a fairly good test. | Grow a frequency tree |
| [L2-06 · See a reversed comparison](#lesson-l2-06) | One option can win inside every group and still lose when the groups are added together. This happens when the options get very different mixes of easy and hard cases. Which comparison to trust depends on the causal story behind the numbers. | Find the flip, then build your own |
| [L2-07 · Read a causal graph](#lesson-l2-07) | A causal graph is a map of what we think causes what. Three small shapes appear again and again: a chain, a fork, and a collider. Looking only inside one group can hide a link in a chain or a fork, but it can create a fake link at a collider. | Sort the stories, then play the admissions game |
| [L2-08 · Observe or intervene](#lesson-l2-08) | Watching who chose something and assigning it to people answer different questions. When a common cause pushes both the choice and the result, the watched gap can be much bigger than the real effect. A coin that decides who gets what removes that push. | Watch, then assign |
| [L2-09 · Use vectors for two outcomes](#lesson-l2-09) | A probability vector (p0, p1) lists two chances that add to 1, so it sits on a line segment. A qubit state vector (a0, a1) lists two amplitudes whose squares add to 1, so it sits on a circle. Two different state vectors can give the same chances in one measurement and still be different states. | Points on a line, arrows on a circle |
| [L2-10 · Build an evidence record](#lesson-l2-10) | A claim is only as strong as the evidence behind it. An evidence record writes down the claim, where it came from, the calculation, what was assumed, how uncertain it is, and who the data covered. It keeps what was tested apart from what was only assumed. | Write your own evidence record |

## Final project: My evidence record

Over three sittings, the learner investigates a question with a two-way table, draws a causal map, and writes an evidence record that states what the data can and cannot show.

**Pick one question, or make up your own:**

- Among classmates or family members who agree to answer, is having a pet linked to time spent outdoors? (Use first names only, or none.)
- In 100 rolls of two dice, is getting a double independent of getting a sum above 7?
- Use a public table from a textbook or a government statistics site and ask whether a comparison changes when you split it into groups.

**Steps:**

1. Write the question, the variables, and their possible values.
2. Collect or copy the data into a two-way table (at least 40 records).
3. Calculate at least two conditional probabilities and say whether the events look independent.
4. Draw a causal map with at least one other variable that could explain the pattern.
5. Say what experiment, if any, would test the causal claim, and why it might not be possible.
6. Write the evidence record: claim, source, assumptions, calculation, uncertainty, and scope.

:::{warning} Project safety
Survey only people who agree. Record no names or private details.
:::

**How it is checked.** Each part has three steps up. Celebrate every step.

| Part | Getting started | Got it | Can explain it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question and variables | I wrote a question. | I named my variables and their values. | I can say why my question is a statistical question. |
| Calculations | I made a table. | I calculated conditional probabilities correctly. | I can explain what each denominator is. |
| Causal map | I drew arrows. | I drew a map with a possible common cause. | I can say how the map changes what my numbers mean. |
| Evidence record | I stated a conclusion. | I listed my assumptions and uncertainty. | I can say what new data would change my mind. |

## Materials for the whole unit

- Graph paper, a ruler, pencils in three colors
- A calculator or a spreadsheet
- Two coins and two dice
- Index cards or sticky notes
- A computer with a web browser for the optional notebooks (Colab or Binder)

:::{warning} Safety
No special hazards. When collecting data from other people, ask permission, keep names off your records, and never collect private or medical information.
:::

## How progress is checked

Each lesson ends with three check questions, one of which looks back at an earlier lesson. Each activity produces a record (a table, a graph, a calculation, or a causal map). The final project is an evidence record judged with a four-part rubric.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: how this unit is designed
:open:

Like the earlier levels, this unit was planned backward from its outcomes [@ubd_whitepaper], and each lesson moves from recall, to a worked example, to practice, to discussion [@merrill2002], with retrieval and spaced review built in [@dunlosky2013]. Each lesson has a short animation made with Manim Community Edition [@manim_ce], driven by the same numbers as the text. The probability ideas follow OpenStax Introductory Statistics [@openstax_contingency; @openstax_independent; @openstax_trees], Bayes' rule is taught with natural frequencies, which research shows people reason with more accurately [@gigerenzer1995], the reversal lesson draws on a classic admissions study [@bickel1975], and the causal maps follow Pearl's framework [@pearl; @bareinboim].

**Ways to adapt it.**

- Each story comes with an animation and a written description of what it shows.
- Learners can show what they know with a table, a graph, a diagram, a calculation, or a spoken explanation.
- Each lesson has a way to make it easier and a way to go further.
- Optional notebooks let learners run the same calculations in Python.
