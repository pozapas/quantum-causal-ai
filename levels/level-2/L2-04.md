## What you will learn

- I can find a conditional probability from a two-way table by dividing by the right total.
- I can test whether two events are independent by comparing P(A \| B) with P(A).

## Story: Do musicians like math more?

Parsa's school club ran an imagined survey of 200 students. These are made-up numbers, not a real school. Each student answered two questions. Do you play an instrument? Do you like math? Ryan put the answers into a two-way table with a total for every row and column.

Of the 200 students, 60 play an instrument. Of those 60, 36 like math and 24 do not. Of the 140 who play no instrument, 64 like math and 76 do not. Ryan checks the table: 60 + 140 = 200, and 100 students like math in all.

Parsa asks a sharper question. "If I only look at the students who play an instrument, what fraction like math?" Now the 60 players are the whole group. So the answer is 36 out of 60, which is 0.6. In symbols, P(math \| instrument) = 36 / 60 = 0.6. The bar means "given".

Ryan compares this with the whole survey. There, 100 out of 200 is 0.5, so P(math) = 0.5. Knowing that a student plays an instrument changes the chance from 0.5 to 0.6. So in this survey, liking math and playing an instrument are not independent.

"So playing music makes you like math?" Ryan asks. Parsa shakes his head. "The table only shows that the two go together here. It does not say which one causes the other, or whether something else causes both." The rule they used is P(A \| B) = P(A and B) / P(B), as long as P(B) is more than 0.

## Hands-on: Doubles and big sums

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- Two dice.
- A paper cup to shake them in (optional).
- Paper and a pencil.
- A calculator (optional).

**Steps:**

1. Draw a two-way table. The rows are "double" and "not a double". The columns are "sum above 7" and "sum 7 or less". Add a total row and a total column.
2. Roll both dice. A double means both dice show the same number. Make a tally mark in the right cell.
3. Roll at least 36 times. Then fill in all the totals and check that they add up to your number of rolls.
4. Find P(double) from your counts: all doubles divided by all rolls.
5. Find P(double \| sum above 7): doubles with a sum above 7, divided by the total of the "sum above 7" column.
6. Now find the exact answers. List all 36 equally likely pairs, from (1,1) to (6,6). Count the doubles and the pairs with a sum above 7.
7. Compare the exact P(double) with the exact P(double \| sum above 7). Are the two events independent? Then compare your counts with the exact answers.

:::{tip} Record it
Your tally table with totals, your two fractions from rolling, the two exact fractions, and one sentence on whether the events are independent.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Keep small dice away from young children who might put them in their mouths. Roll on a table or a tray so the dice do not fall.
:::

## Practice together

Use the 36 equally likely pairs from two dice. What is P(double)? What is P(double \| sum above 7)? Are the two events independent?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
There are 6 doubles, so P(double) = 6/36 = 1/6. There are 15 pairs with a sum above 7. Only 3 of them are doubles: (4,4), (5,5) and (6,6). So P(double \| sum above 7) = 3/15 = 1/5. Since 1/5 is not 1/6, the events are not independent.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** In another imagined class of 40 students (made-up numbers), 20 have a pet. Of the 16 who walk to school, 8 have a pet. Find P(pet) and P(pet \| walks). Are the two events independent in this class?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
P(pet) = 20/40 = 0.5. P(pet \| walks) = 8/16 = 0.5. They are equal, so in this class having a pet and walking to school are independent.
:::

**2.** Roll two dice. Event A is "a double". Event B is "an odd sum". Can A and B happen together? Are they independent?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
A double always has an even sum, so A and B can never happen together. They are mutually exclusive. They are not independent: P(A) = 1/6, but P(A \| B) = 0. Knowing B happened tells you A did not.
:::

**1. In the imagined survey, what is P(math \| instrument)?**

- **A.** 36 out of 200
- **B.** 36 out of 100
- **C.** 36 out of 60

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. 36 out of 60**

The condition is "plays an instrument", so only the 60 players count. Of them, 36 like math, which is 36 out of 60.
:::

**2. For two events with P(B) more than 0, which statement shows they are independent?**

- **A.** A and B can never happen together.
- **B.** P(A \| B) = P(A)
- **C.** P(A and B) = P(B)

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. P(A \| B) = P(A)**

Independent means knowing B does not change the chance of A, so P(A \| B) = P(A). Events that never happen together are mutually exclusive, which is different.

## Words to know

**conditional probability**
: The chance of one event when we already know a second event happened. We write P(A \| B) and say "the probability of A given B".

**independent**
: Two events are independent when knowing that one happened does not change the chance of the other. In symbols, P(A \| B) = P(A).

**mutually exclusive**
: Two events are mutually exclusive when they can never happen together, like rolling a 2 and rolling a 5 on one roll of a die.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** The lesson builds conditional probability from a two-way table, where the row or column total is visibly the denominator. Two-way tables and conditional probability are presented this way in OpenStax [@openstax_contingency], and independence is defined there as P(A \| B) = P(A), separate from mutual exclusivity [@openstax_independent]. The standards ask students to read two-way tables (8.SP.4) and to understand conditional probability as P(A and B) / P(B) and independence as P(A \| B) = P(A) (S-CP.3) [@ccss_math]. The survey is made up and is labeled so on every surface. The dice task gives an exact check: 3 of the 15 pairs with a sum above 7 are doubles, so 1/5, not 1/6.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- P(A \| B) and P(B \| A) are the same. (They have different denominators; in the survey, 36 of 60 players like math, but 36 of 100 math-likers play.)
- Mutually exclusive means independent. (If two possible events cannot happen together, knowing one rules out the other, so they are dependent.)
- A difference in a table shows a cause. (It shows an association in this group only; a common cause or chance could explain it.)
- Rolled counts that give slightly different fractions disprove the exact answer. (Thirty-six rolls vary a lot; pooled rolls get closer to 1/6 and 1/5.)

**To make it easier.** Give the completed survey table and ask only for P(math) and P(math \| instrument). Skip the dice table and use the list of 36 pairs.

**To make it harder.** Find P(instrument \| math) from the same table and explain why it differs from P(math \| instrument). Then change one cell so the events become independent while all totals still add up.

**Limits.** The survey numbers are invented for teaching. A real survey of 200 students estimates a pattern with sampling error, so two fractions that differ a little could still come from independent events in the wider population.
