## What you will learn

- I can build a frequency tree for 1,000 people from a base rate and two test rates.
- I can find P(condition \| positive) by dividing true positives by all positives.
- I can explain why a rare condition gives many false positives.

## Story: A positive test at Maya's school

Maya's imagined school has 1,000 students. It runs an imagined screening test for a rare, made-up allergy. All the numbers in this story are made up. The base rate is 1 percent, so 10 have the allergy and 990 do not.

The test catches 90 percent of people who have the allergy. So 9 of the 10 test positive, and 1 is missed. The test also has a false positive rate of 9 percent. That sounds small, but 9 percent of 990 is 89.1. So about 89 without the allergy also test positive.

Leo's friend Priya gets a positive result and panics. "The test is 90 percent right, so I almost surely have it!" Maya draws the counts. About 98 students test positive in all: the 9 true positives plus about 89 false ones. Only 9 of them have the allergy.

So P(allergy \| positive) is 9 out of 98, about 9 percent. The 90 percent was a different number, P(positive \| allergy). It answers the question "of the students with the allergy, how many test positive?" Priya's question was the reverse: "of the students who test positive, how many have the allergy?"

The reason is the base rate. The 990 students without the allergy are a huge group, so even a small error rate makes many false alarms. Counting this way is Bayes' rule in disguise. The short version is P(allergy \| positive) = true positives / all positives.

## Hands-on: Grow a frequency tree

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- Paper and a pencil.
- A ruler.
- A calculator (optional).
- Two colored pens (optional).

**Steps:**

1. At the top of the page, write 1,000 students. This is an imagined school with made-up numbers.
2. Draw two branches down. Label one "has the allergy" and the other "does not". Use the base rate of 1 percent to write 10 and 990.
3. Under "has the allergy", draw two branches: "positive" and "negative". The test catches 90 percent, so write 9 and 1.
4. Under "does not", draw the same two branches. The false positive rate is 9 percent. Work out 9 percent of 990 and round to a whole person.
5. Circle every positive count. Add them to find all the positives.
6. Write P(allergy \| positive) as true positives out of all positives. Change it to a percentage.
7. Now draw a new tree with a base rate of 10 percent. Keep the same test rates. Compare the two answers.

:::{tip} Record it
Your two frequency trees, the fraction P(allergy \| positive) for each one as a fraction and a percentage, and one sentence on what changed.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
This is a made-up test for a made-up allergy. It is not medical advice, and real test results should be explained by a doctor.
:::

## Practice together

Redo the tree with a base rate of 10 percent. The test still catches 90 percent and has a false positive rate of 9 percent. What is P(allergy \| positive)?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Of 1,000 students, 100 have the allergy and 900 do not. The test catches 90 of the 100. It also flags 9 percent of 900, which is 81. That makes 90 + 81 = 171 positives. So P(allergy \| positive) = 90/171, about 53 percent. The test did not change. Only the base rate did.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** An imagined test (made-up numbers) is used on 1,000 people. The base rate is 2 percent. The test catches 90 percent of cases and has a false positive rate of 5 percent. Find P(condition \| positive).

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
20 people have the condition and 980 do not. The test catches 18 of the 20. It also flags 5 percent of 980, which is 49. There are 18 + 49 = 67 positives, so the answer is 18/67, about 27 percent.
:::

**2.** A friend says, "The test catches 90 percent of cases, so a positive means a 90 percent chance." Which conditional probability is your friend mixing up?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
The 90 percent is P(positive \| condition). The friend wants P(condition \| positive). These have different groups on the bottom, so they can be far apart. You need the base rate to get from one to the other.
:::

:::{seealso} Where this leads
Doctors, spam filters and fraud checkers all face this same base rate problem. Many AI systems use Bayes' rule to update a chance when new evidence arrives. Later lessons use the same habit: always ask which group is on the bottom of the fraction.

## Words to know

**base rate**
: How common something is in the whole group before any test. For example, 1 percent of students.

**false positive**
: A test result that says "yes" for someone who does not have the condition.

**natural frequencies**
: Counts of people, like "9 out of 98", used in place of percentages so the groups stay easy to see.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** The lesson teaches Bayes' rule through natural frequencies: a frequency tree of 1,000 people in place of the formula. Gigerenzer and Hoffrage found that natural frequency formats make this kind of reasoning much more accurate than single-event probabilities [@gigerenzer1995]. Tree diagrams for conditional events follow OpenStax [@openstax_trees], and finding probabilities of compound events with trees and tables matches 7.SP.8 [@ccss_math]. The numbers come from the course function bayes(0.01, 0.9, 0.09): positives are 0.0981 of the group (98.1 per 1,000), and the posterior is about 0.092. The lesson rounds 89.1 false positives to about 89, so it says 9 out of 98.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- A test that catches 90 percent of cases gives a 90 percent chance after a positive. (That confuses P(positive \| condition) with P(condition \| positive); the base rate matters.)
- A small false positive rate means few false positives. (A small rate times a huge group of people without the condition can still be many people.)
- A positive test with a low posterior means the test is useless. (The chance rose from 1 percent to about 9 percent; a second, separate test could raise it further.)

**To make it easier.** Do only the 1 percent tree with the counts already filled in at the first level, and ask for the final fraction 9 out of 98.

**To make it harder.** Write the general formula P(A \| B) = P(B \| A) P(A) / P(B) and check it against the tree. Then find the base rate at which half of all positives are true, using the same test rates.

**Limits.** The test, the allergy and every rate here are invented. Real screening tests have rates that vary by group and setting, and real results need a doctor to interpret them. Rounding to whole people changes the answer by about 0.0001 (9/98 is 0.0918; the exact value is 0.0917).
