## Story: The juice poster

Ryan spots a poster for Brand X juice. It is made up for this lesson, but it looks like a real advert: "Students who drink Brand X get better grades!" Parsa grins. "Let's build an evidence record and see what holds up."

They imagine a survey with made-up numbers: 80 students at one imagined school, over one term. Of 30 juice drinkers, 18 out of 30 had mostly A and B grades. That is 60 percent. Of the 50 other students, 20 out of 50 did. That is 40 percent. The gap is 20 points.

Next, Parsa draws a causal map. "What if students who sleep well and eat breakfast also drink more juice? Sleep could raise grades on its own." The survey cannot rule that out. So "no common cause" is an assumption, not something the data tested.

Ryan adds two more worries. "Only 80 students is a small sample. Another school could show a smaller gap, or none." And the scope is narrow: one imagined school, one term. The poster talks about all students everywhere.

They finish the record. Tested: a gap of 20 points in made-up data. Assumed: no common cause. Not tested: whether juice causes better grades. "To test that," says Parsa, "a coin would decide who gets the juice, like the app study in Lesson 8."

## Hands-on: Write your own evidence record

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- A printed or hand-drawn template with six rows: claim, source, calculation, assumptions, uncertainty, scope
- A pencil
- Your notes from Lessons 3, 5, 6, and 8
- A calculator (optional)

**Steps:**

1. Choose a claim. Use one from Lessons 3, 5, 6, or 8, or a headline from a news site or an advert. Copy it word for word.
2. Source: write where the claim came from and who collected the data. Say whether the numbers are real or made up.
3. Calculation: copy or redo the key number, such as a rate, a conditional probability, or a gap. Write each denominator.
4. Draw a causal map for the claim. Include at least one possible common cause.
5. Assumptions: list what the claim needs to be true that the data did not test.
6. Uncertainty: write how many records there were. Could chance or a small sample explain the result?
7. Scope: write who, where, and when the data covered. Then rewrite the claim so it stays inside that scope.
8. Finish with one line: what experiment would test the claim, or why one is not possible.

:::{tip} Record it
Your completed evidence record with all six rows, your causal map, and your rewritten claim.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Do not collect or write down personal information about anyone. Ask an adult before searching online for a headline.
:::

## Check yourself

**1. In the juice record, which item was assumed rather than tested?**

- **A.** That 18 out of 30 drinkers had mostly A and B grades
- **B.** That no common cause, such as sleep, affects both juice and grades
- **C.** That 80 students took part

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. That no common cause, such as sleep, affects both juice and grades**

The counts came from the made-up survey. Whether a common cause is at work was never checked, so it is an assumption.
:::

**2. Which scope statement fits the made-up juice data best?**

- **A.** All students everywhere
- **B.** Every student who has ever tried Brand X
- **C.** 80 students, one imagined school, one term

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. 80 students, one imagined school, one term**

A scope should name exactly who, where, and when the data covered, and no more.
:::

**3. Remember Lesson 6. In made-up data, program A did better than B in easy courses and in hard courses, but worse overall. What usually causes a flip like this?** *(Review of L2-06)*

- **A.** Someone made an adding mistake.
- **B.** The groups had different sizes, because A took mostly hard courses.
- **C.** A is really worse in every course.

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. The groups had different sizes, because A took mostly hard courses.**

When most of A's students took hard courses, A's combined rate is pulled down. The split shows A did better in each group.

## Words to know

**evidence record**
: A short write-up of a claim with its source, calculation, assumptions, uncertainty, and scope.

**assumption**
: Something a claim needs to be true but that the data did not test.

**scope**
: Who, where, and when the data covered. A claim should not reach past its scope.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** This capstone lesson practices two NGSS science and engineering practices: constructing explanations and engaging in argument from evidence [@ngss_appf]. The assumptions row draws on causal inference: an association in observational data has a causal reading only under assumptions such as no unmeasured common cause, which a randomized experiment makes far more plausible [@hernan]. Pearl stresses writing causal assumptions down explicitly, for example as a graph, so others can examine them [@pearl]. The juice survey is entirely made up, and its numbers (18 of 30 against 20 of 50) are stored with the animation so the text and video match.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- A big gap in a table proves the claim. (A gap shows an association in that data; a cause needs more, such as a randomized trial or a well-argued causal map.)
- Assumptions are weaknesses to hide. (Every claim rests on some; writing them down lets others check them.)
- A result from one school applies everywhere. (Keep the claim inside the scope the data covered.)
- Uncertainty means the result is useless. (It says how far to trust the result and what extra data would help.)

**To make it easier.** Fill in only four rows: claim, source, calculation, and scope. Use the juice example and change only the numbers.

**To make it harder.** Write evidence records for two claims, one from an experiment and one from watched data, and compare their assumptions rows. Then swap with a partner and challenge one assumption each.

**Limits.** The juice poster and survey are invented. Real headlines can come from strong or weak studies, and a short record cannot replace reading the original study.
