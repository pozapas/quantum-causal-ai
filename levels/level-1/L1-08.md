## Story: When two bumps meet

Parsa and Ryan stretch a long rope between them. Each one flicks a single bump up toward the other at the same moment. On their graph paper, each bump is 2 squares tall. In the middle, the bumps meet. For an instant, the rope jumps up 4 squares, because 2 plus 2 is 4. Then the bumps pass through each other and keep going.

Next, Parsa flicks a bump up and Ryan flicks a bump down, each 2 squares. When they meet, the rope looks flat for an instant. An up of 2 and a down of 2 cancel. The bumps did not break. A moment later, they come out the other side.

Back at the table, Ryan draws a wave on tracing paper, with crests 2 squares high. Parsa draws the same wave on another sheet. They lay one on top of the other, in step. At every point they add the heights: 2 up and 2 up make 4 up, and 2 down and 2 down make 4 down. The new wave has twice the amplitude.

Then Parsa slides his sheet half a cycle, 4 squares along. Now each crest sits on a trough. At every point, the two heights are equal and opposite, like up 2 and down 2, so they add to 0. The two waves cancel completely.

Their teacher adds one more rule. For light, brightness goes with the amplitude times itself. Say one light wave has amplitude 1. Then amplitude 1 gives brightness 1, and amplitude 2 gives brightness 4. In step, the brightness is 4 times one wave, not 2 times. Half a cycle apart, amplitude 0 gives brightness 0. The extra light in bright places comes from dark places nearby, where waves cancel.

## Hands-on: Add two waves on paper

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- Graph paper
- Two sheets of tracing paper or thin printer paper
- A pencil and a colored pencil
- A ruler
- A rope and a partner (optional)

**Steps:**

1. On graph paper, draw a long middle line. Mark a dot on it every 2 squares.
2. Lay a sheet of tracing paper on top. Draw a wave with crests 2 squares high and 8 squares apart. Start at the middle line on the first dot.
3. On the second sheet, draw the same wave in a different color.
4. Lay both sheets on the graph paper, in step. At each dot, add the two heights. Count heights above the line as up and below it as down. Draw the sum on the graph paper.
5. Slide the top sheet 4 squares along, which is half a cycle. Add the heights at each dot again and draw this second sum.
6. Make a table with three rows: one wave, two waves in step, and two waves half a cycle apart. For each row, write the amplitude of the sum and that amplitude times itself.
7. Optional: hold a rope with a partner. Each flick one bump at the same moment, both up, then one up and one down. Watch what happens where they meet.

:::{tip} Record it
Your two drawn sums and your table of amplitudes and squares.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Keep pencils pointed down when you lean over the paper. If you use a rope, keep it low and never wrap it around a neck or a hand.
:::

## Practice together

At four dots, wave A has heights 0, 2 up, 0, 2 down. Wave B is in step and has the same heights. What are the heights of the sum? What if wave B is half a cycle behind instead?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
In step, the sum is 0, 4 up, 0, 4 down, a wave with amplitude 4. Half a cycle behind, wave B has heights 0, 2 down, 0, 2 up, so the sum is 0 at every dot. The waves cancel.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** At one point, wave A is 3 squares up and wave B is 1 square down. What is the height of the sum there?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Up 3 plus down 1 is up 2. The sum is 2 squares up at that point.
:::

**2.** A light wave has amplitude 3, so its brightness is 3 times 3, which is 9. Two of these waves meet in step. What is the new amplitude? What is the new brightness? How many times as bright as one wave is that?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
The amplitudes add to 6. The brightness is 6 times 6, which is 36. That is 4 times as bright as one wave, because 36 is 4 times 9.
:::

## Talk about it

- When the up bump and the down bump met, the rope looked flat. Where did the bumps go?
- Why must you add the heights first and square after? What goes wrong if you square each wave first?
- Two waves in step make a spot 4 times as bright. Why does that not mean light was made from nothing?

## Check yourself

**1. Two equal light waves meet in step. How bright is the result, compared with one wave?**

- **A.** The same brightness
- **B.** 2 times as bright
- **C.** 4 times as bright

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. 4 times as bright**

The amplitudes add, so the total amplitude is 2 times as big. Brightness goes with amplitude times itself, and 2 times 2 is 4.
:::

**2. Two equal waves meet half a cycle apart. What happens?**

- **A.** They make a wave twice as tall
- **B.** They make a wave half as tall
- **C.** They cancel, so the sum is flat

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. They cancel, so the sum is flat**

Half a cycle apart, each crest meets a trough of the same size. Up plus an equal down is 0 at every point, so the waves cancel.
:::

:::{seealso} Where this leads
The same order, add first and square after, comes back later in this course. In quantum physics, chances come from amplitudes. Amplitudes add first, then you square the total to get a chance. Ordinary chances can only add up, but amplitudes can cancel.
:::

## Words to know

**amplitude**
: How far a crest rises above the middle line.

**cancel**
: To add up to zero. An up of 2 and a down of 2 cancel.

**square**
: To square a number, multiply it by itself. The square of 3 is 3 times 3, which is 9.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** OpenStax describes superposition: where waves overlap, the resulting displacement is the sum of the individual displacements, giving constructive interference when they are in phase and destructive interference when they are half a cycle out of phase [@openstax_interference]. A standard result of wave physics is that for light and sound the intensity (energy carried per second per area) is proportional to the square of the amplitude, so two equal waves in phase give twice the amplitude and four times the intensity of one. The order matters: adding amplitudes first and then squaring is not the same as squaring each and adding. The quantum preview in the Where this leads paragraph follows Feynman's account of the two-slit experiment, where the chance of arrival is found by adding the amplitudes for the two paths first and then taking the absolute square [@feynman]. The animation computes each wave with numpy and draws the sum as exactly the pointwise sum of the two drawn waves.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- Two waves in step give twice the brightness. (They give twice the amplitude and four times the brightness.)
- When waves cancel, they are destroyed. (They pass through each other and continue; the flat moment is the sum at that time and place.)
- Interference makes extra energy at bright spots. (Light is moved, not made: bright places are balanced by dark places nearby.)
- You can square each wave first and then add. (That gives 1 + 1 = 2 for two equal waves in step and can never give 0; add the amplitudes first.)

**To make it easier.** Do only the in-step and half-cycle overlays at four dots. Use whole numbers and skip the brightness table, or do just one row of it.

**To make it harder.** Slide the top sheet a quarter cycle (2 squares) and add again. The sum is a wave in between in size. Measure its amplitude and square it to compare with the other cases.

**Limits.** "Brightness goes with amplitude squared" is about intensity, the energy carried by the wave. How bright or loud something seems to a person grows more slowly than that. Two ordinary flashlights do not show light and dark stripes, because their waves do not keep a steady step with each other. Seeing interference with light usually uses a single source split into two paths, such as two narrow slits.
