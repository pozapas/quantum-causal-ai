## What you will learn

- I can write a fraction for part of a strip or part of a group.
- I can show that one half, two fourths, and four eighths are the same amount.
- I can say what the whole is before I write a fraction.

:::{note} Warm up
Think of a pizza cut into 4 equal slices. You eat 1 slice. How many slices are left? How would you say how much of the pizza you ate?
:::

## Story: Sharing a strip and a bag of marbles

Parsa and Ryan have one long strip of paper. They fold it in half and color one part. One part is colored, and there are 2 equal parts in all. That is 1 out of 2, or one half.

Ryan folds the strip in half again. Now there are 4 equal parts, and 2 of them are colored. That is 2 out of 4. Parsa folds it once more. Now there are 8 equal parts, and 4 are colored: 4 out of 8. "The colored part never changed," says Ryan. "We only cut it into smaller pieces." So 1 out of 2, 2 out of 4, and 4 out of 8 are the same amount.

Next they open a bag of 12 marbles. 3 of the marbles are green. Ryan writes 3 out of 12 as a fraction: 3/12. The numerator, 3, counts the green marbles. The denominator, 12, counts all the marbles.

Parsa puts the marbles into 4 equal groups of 3. He puts the 3 green marbles together in one group. Now 1 out of 4 groups is green. So 3 out of 12 is the same as 1 out of 4.

"What if the bag had only 6 marbles, with 3 green?" asks Ryan. Then 3 out of 6 would be green, which is one half. The part is the same, but the whole is smaller. That is why you always say what the whole is.

## Hands-on: Fold, shade, and group

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- Three strips of paper, all the same length
- Colored pencils or crayons
- A ruler
- 12 counters, beans, or buttons, with 3 of one color

**Steps:**

1. Fold the first strip in half. Open it and shade one part, starting from the left end. Write 1/2 on it.
2. Fold the second strip in half, then in half again. Open it. Shade 2 parts next to each other from the left end. Write 2/4.
3. Fold the third strip in half three times. Open it. Shade 4 parts next to each other from the left end. Write 4/8.
4. Line up the three strips, one under the other. Use the ruler to check that the shaded parts are the same length.
5. Put out the 12 counters. Write a fraction for the 3 counters of the other color.
6. Sort the counters into 4 equal groups of 3. Put the 3 counters of the other color together in one group. Write the new fraction for the groups.
7. Write two fractions that name the same amount, like 3/12 and 1/4.

:::{tip} Record it
Your three shaded strips with their fractions, a drawing of your 12 counters in 4 groups, and the two fractions that match.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Keep counters, beans, and buttons away from babies and toddlers. Never put beans in your mouth, nose, or ears.
:::

## Practice together

A tray has 10 pieces of fruit. 2 are pears and the rest are apples. What fraction of the fruit is pears? What fraction is apples?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
The whole is all 10 pieces of fruit. Pears are 2 out of 10, or 2/10. Apples are the other 8, so they are 8 out of 10, or 8/10. Together, the two parts make the whole tray.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** A box has 6 red blocks and 9 blue blocks. What fraction of the blocks are red?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
There are 15 blocks in all. The red blocks are 6 out of 15, or 6/15. The bottom number counts all the blocks, not just the blue ones.
:::

**2.** Ryan shades 3 parts of a strip folded into 4 equal parts. Parsa shades 6 parts of a strip folded into 8 equal parts. Did they shade the same amount?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Yes. Ryan shaded 3/4 of his strip. Parsa shaded 6/8. Each of Ryan's parts is the same as two of Parsa's parts, so 3/4 and 6/8 are the same amount.
:::

## Check yourself

**1. A class has 20 students, and 5 of them are left-handed. What fraction of the class is left-handed?**

- **A.** 20/5
- **B.** 5/20
- **C.** 5/15

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. 5/20**

The whole class has 20 students, and the part is the 5 who are left-handed. So the fraction is 5 out of 20, or 5/20.
:::

**2. Which fraction is the same amount as one half?**

- **A.** 4/8
- **B.** 1/4
- **C.** 2/8

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**A. 4/8**

A strip folded into 8 equal parts with 4 shaded is half shaded. So 4/8 is the same amount as one half.
:::

**3. In a fraction, what does the denominator count?**

- **A.** The part you are looking at
- **B.** The parts that are left over
- **C.** All the equal parts in the whole

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. All the equal parts in the whole**

The denominator is the bottom number. It counts all the equal parts in the whole. The numerator, on top, counts the part.
:::

## Words to know

**fraction**
: A number that compares a part with a whole, like 3 out of 12, written 3/12.

**numerator**
: The top number of a fraction. It counts the part you are looking at.

**denominator**
: The bottom number of a fraction. It counts all the equal parts in the whole.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** The lesson builds the two fraction ideas of the Common Core that the rest of Level 1 needs: a fraction as a part of a whole cut into equal parts (3.NF.1), and equal fractions shown with a visual model, such as folded strips (4.NF.1) [@ccss_math]. Later lessons turn counts into probabilities and conditional fractions, which only make sense if the child first asks what the whole is.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- A bigger bottom number means a bigger fraction. (Eighths are smaller pieces than halves, so 1/8 is less than 1/2.)
- Any split into 4 pieces makes fourths. (The pieces must be equal for each to be one fourth.)
- 3 green marbles is always the same fraction. (It depends on the whole: 3 out of 12 is not 3 out of 6.)
- Equal fractions must use the same numbers. (2/4 and 4/8 use different numbers but name the same amount.)

**To make it easier.** Use only halves and fourths, and a group of 4 counters with 1 of a different color.

**To make it harder.** Fold a strip into thirds and then sixths, and find a fraction equal to 2/3. Or sort 12 counters with 4 of one color into equal groups, and show that 4/12 is the same as 1/3.

**Limits.** Fractions of a group describe that group only. A bag with 3 green marbles out of 12 says nothing about another bag. The lesson uses small whole numbers and only equal parts; it does not yet cover comparing fractions by size.
