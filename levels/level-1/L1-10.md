## What you will learn

- I can find the chance that two dice add to 7 by listing all 36 pairs.
- I can write a model card with a prediction and a result that would make me doubt the model.
- I can compare my real counts with my prediction without changing the rule afterward.

## Story: Testing the dice

Parsa and Ryan want to know how often two dice add up to 7. They list every way the dice can land. The first die can show 1 to 6, and so can the second. That makes 36 pairs, and with fair dice each pair is equally likely.

Six of the pairs add to 7. They are 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4, 4 and 3, 5 and 2, and 6 and 1. So their model says the chance of a 7 is 6 out of 36, which is one sixth. In 36 rolls, they predict about 6 sevens.

Before rolling, they write a model card. "What would make us doubt the dice?" asks Ryan. They agree on a rule: 0 or 1 sevens, or 12 or more. With fair dice, a result that far off happens only about 2 times in 100.

Then they roll 36 times and tally every sum. They get 3 sevens in 36 rolls. That is 3 out of 36, fewer than 6. Ryan groans. "Is the model wrong?"

Parsa points to the card. "We said we would doubt it at 1 or fewer. Three is lower than we guessed, but it is not past our line. We have no reason to doubt the dice yet." They write what happened on the card, without changing the rule.

## Hands-on: My model card for two dice

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- Two dice
- A cup to shake them in (optional)
- Paper and a pencil
- A ruler for drawing a table

**Steps:**

1. Draw a 6 by 6 grid. Write 1 to 6 across the top for the first die and down the side for the second die.
2. Fill each square with the sum of its two numbers. Circle every square that shows 7. How many did you circle?
3. Write your model card. Question: how often do two dice add to 7? Assumption: the dice are fair.
4. Add your prediction to the card: about 6 sevens in 36 rolls.
5. Add your doubt rule before you roll: 0 or 1 sevens, or 12 or more.
6. Write the sums 2 to 12 down the side of a new page. Roll both dice 36 times and make a tally mark next to each sum.
7. Count your 7s. Write the result on the card under what happened.
8. Compare with your doubt rule. Do you have a reason to doubt the dice? Do not change the rule now.

:::{tip} Record it
Your 6 by 6 grid, your tally of 36 rolls, and your finished model card.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
Keep dice away from babies and toddlers. Roll on a table or a tray so the dice do not get lost.
:::

## Practice together

Using the 6 by 6 grid, how many pairs add up to 2? What is the chance of rolling a sum of 2?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Only one pair adds to 2: a 1 and a 1. So the chance is 1 out of 36. A sum of 7 is six times as likely.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** Amin says, "My lucky model says something will happen." Amin will not say what or when. Is this a good model? Why or why not?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
No. No test could ever prove it wrong, because almost anything counts as something happening. A good model makes a clear prediction that could turn out false.
:::

**2.** Ryan rolls two dice 36 times and gets 0 sevens. Using the rule on the card, what should he write under what happened?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Zero is inside the doubt rule of 0 or 1 sevens. He should write that he got 0 sevens and that he now doubts the model. Next he could check the dice and roll again.
:::

## Talk about it

- Why should you write your doubt rule before you roll, not after?
- Parsa and Ryan got 3 sevens, not 6. Why is that not enough to call the dice unfair?
- If four friends each roll two dice 36 times, will they all get the same number of sevens? Why not?

## Check yourself

**1. Which of these is a testable prediction?**

- **A.** Dice are fun to roll.
- **B.** In 36 rolls of two fair dice, we will get about 6 sevens.
- **C.** Something interesting might happen someday.

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. In 36 rolls of two fair dice, we will get about 6 sevens.**

This prediction gives a number that a test can check. If the count were very far from 6, the test would make us doubt the model.
:::

**2. Your doubt rule is 0 or 1 sevens, or 12 or more. You roll 36 times and get 9 sevens. What should you do?**

- **A.** Change the rule so 9 counts as too many
- **B.** Throw out the rolls and start over
- **C.** Write down 9 sevens and say there is no reason to doubt the dice yet

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**C. Write down 9 sevens and say there is no reason to doubt the dice yet**

Nine is not past either line of your rule, so you keep the model for now. Changing the rule after you see the result would not be a fair test.
:::

**3. Remember Lesson 5. Why do scientists flip a coin to choose who goes in which group?** *(Review of L1-05)*

:::{seealso} Where this leads
Careful scientists often write down their predictions and doubt rules before they collect data. Then nobody can move the line after seeing the results. You can write a model card for any test, from dice to paper planes.
:::

## Words to know

**model card**
: A short record of a model: the question, what you assume, what you predict, what would make you doubt it, and what happened.

**prediction**
: What you expect to happen before you test, based on your model.

**assumption**
: Something you take to be true so your model can work, like the dice being fair.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** The Common Core asks students to build a probability model, here 36 equally likely ordered pairs, and to compare its predictions with observed frequencies, explaining possible reasons for a mismatch (7.SP.7) [@ccss_math]. OpenStax defines equally likely outcomes and relative frequency, and states the law of large numbers: the relative frequency tends toward the theoretical probability as the number of trials grows, so a short run of 36 rolls can still land some way off [@openstax_prob]. The NGSS practices ask children to make predictions, plan investigations, and use evidence to judge an explanation [@ngss_appf]. The doubt rule of 0 or 1 sevens, or 12 or more, is a two-sided cutoff: with fair dice, the binomial chance of a count that far off in 36 rolls is about 0.023. The roll sequence comes from the course's seeded random number generator (seed 1), so the 3 sevens in the story, the video and this guide match.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- If the count is not exactly 6, the model is wrong. (Counts vary; 3 sevens in 36 rolls is not unusual for fair dice.)
- A test that agrees with the model proves it is true. (It only means the test gave no reason to doubt it; a later test still could.)
- It is fine to choose the doubt rule after seeing the results. (Choosing it afterward lets you excuse any result; set it in advance.)
- A sum of 7 has one way to happen, 3 and 4. (Order matters on two dice: 3 then 4 and 4 then 3 are different pairs, so there are 6 ways.)

**To make it easier.** Use 18 rolls and predict about 3 sevens. Skip the doubt rule numbers and simply ask: would 0 sevens surprise you?

**To make it harder.** Pool the 36-roll tallies from several people. Plot how many sevens each person got, and see that most counts land between 3 and 9. Then write a model card for the sum of 2, which has a chance of 1 out of 36.

**Limits.** Passing a test does not prove a model is true, and one unusual result does not prove it false. About 2 times in 100, fair dice will break this doubt rule by chance alone.
