**The big question:** How do we describe chance, test fairly, and tell what waves can do that coins cannot?

Learners turn counts into fractions and probabilities, compare what a model predicts with what really happens, and use random assignment to make a fair comparison. Then they meet waves, which can add up or cancel. That idea, adding first and then squaring, is the first step toward quantum physics.
:::

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Who it is for | Learners aged about 9 to 12 who have finished Level 0 or can count, compare, and read simple tables. They can work through most of a lesson alone. A grown-up or teacher checks the record sheet and joins the talk questions. |
| Lessons | 10 lessons of about 40 minutes, plus a 3-part final project |
| Pace | Two lessons a week takes about six weeks, including the final project. Any lesson can be split into two sittings: story, animation and activity one day, practice and checks the next. |
| Total sittings | 13 |

## How a lesson works

1. Warm up: answer the look-back question from memory.
2. Read the story and watch the animation.
3. Do the hands-on activity and keep a record.
4. Practice together, then try two on your own.
5. Talk about it, then answer the three check questions. Say or write your answer before you open it.
6. Grown-ups: read the short guide at the end of the page.

## Lessons

| Lesson | Big idea | Hands-on |
|---|---|---|
| [L1-01 · Fractions describe part of a whole](#lesson-l1-01) | A fraction compares a part with the whole. The top number counts the part. The bottom number counts all the equal parts in the whole. Different fractions can name the same amount. | Fold, shade, and group |
| [L1-02 · Build a probability model](#lesson-l1-02) | A probability model lists everything that could happen and how likely each one is. When every outcome is equally likely, the probability of something happening is the number of outcomes that count, out of all the outcomes. A probability goes from 0, which means impossible, to 1, which means certain. | Three probability models |
| [L1-03 · Repeat and record](#lesson-l1-03) | A probability is what a model predicts. A frequency is what you actually count. With more trials, the frequency usually gets closer to the probability, but it rarely lands on it exactly. | Coin flip record |
| [L1-04 · Count within a condition](#lesson-l1-04) | When a question says "of the squares" or "among the kids with glasses", it picks out a smaller group. That group becomes the whole. The bottom number of your fraction is the size of that group, not the size of everything. | Shape sort and two-way table |
| [L1-05 · Assign a fair comparison](#lesson-l1-05) | To test two things fairly, let chance decide who gets which. A coin does not care who is strong or weak. So hidden differences between the groups balance out on average, but not perfectly every time. | Coin groups or picked groups? |
| [L1-06 · Look for a common cause](#lesson-l1-06) | Two things can go up and down together even when neither one causes the other. Often a third thing causes both. Looking only at the pattern cannot tell you which way the causes run. | Find the hidden cause |
| [L1-07 · Track a wave](#lesson-l1-07) | A wave is a pattern of ups and downs that travels. Its amplitude is how high a crest rises above the middle line. Its wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next. The rope itself only moves up and down. | Rope waves |
| [L1-08 · Combine waves, then square](#lesson-l1-08) | When two waves meet, their heights add at every point. In step, they make a bigger wave. Half a cycle apart and the same size, they cancel. For light, brightness goes with the amplitude times itself, so the order matters: add first, then square. | Add two waves on paper |
| [L1-09 · Unknown is not the same as quantum](#lesson-l1-09) | In the two-path experiment with light, some spots stay dark because the amplitudes from the two paths cancel. Ordinary chances can only add up, but amplitudes can cancel. When a coin is hidden under a cup, you do not know how it landed. Not knowing which way a coin landed is different: the coin already landed one way. | Hidden coins and paper arrows |
| [L1-10 · Keep a model testable](#lesson-l1-10) | A good model makes a prediction that a test could prove wrong. Before you test, write down what result would make you doubt the model. Then test it and write down what really happened. | My model card for two dice |

## Final project: My chance and cause investigation

Over three sittings, the learner runs a small experiment with random assignment, compares a model with real counts, and writes a model card.

**Pick one question, or make up your own:**

- Does a paper airplane with folded wing tips fly farther than one without? Flip a coin to decide which plane each throw uses.
- Do two dice add to 7 as often as a model predicts?
- Does a ball roll farther on carpet or on a hard floor? Use a coin to decide the order of the trials.

**Steps:**

1. Pick a question. Write a prediction and the model behind it.
2. Plan a fair test. Use a coin or a die to assign which choice each trial gets.
3. Run at least 20 trials. Record every result in a table.
4. Turn your counts into fractions, and compare them with your model.
5. Make a graph of your results.
6. Write a model card: question, assumptions, prediction, what would prove it wrong, and what happened.

:::{warning} Project safety
Throw paper airplanes away from faces. Roll balls away from stairs. Keep dice away from babies and toddlers.
:::

**How it is checked.** Each part has three steps up. Celebrate every step.

| Part | Getting started | Got it | Can explain it |
|---|---|---|---|
| My question and model | I wrote a question. | I wrote a question and a prediction from a model. | I can say what my model assumes. |
| My fair test | I compared two choices. | I used a coin or die to assign the choices. | I can say why random assignment makes it fair. |
| My record | I wrote some results. | I recorded at least 20 trials and turned them into fractions. | I can say why my fractions are not exactly what the model predicted. |
| My model card | I said what happened. | I compared my results with my prediction. | I named a result that would have proved my model wrong. |

## Materials for the whole unit

- Two coins, two dice, and a paper cup
- Counters, beads, or building bricks in two or three colors, and a paper bag
- Graph paper or a printed grid, a ruler, pencils, and colored pencils
- A calculator (optional)
- A jump rope or a long, soft rope, or a toy spring coil
- A shallow tray or baking dish of water and a small cup
- Paper for paper airplanes or a paper cup tower, and masking tape

:::{warning} Safety
Keep coins, dice, and small counters away from babies and toddlers. Use water trays on a towel, away from screens and plugs, and wipe up spills right away. Swing ropes slowly, with space around you.
:::

## How progress is checked

Progress shows up three ways. Each lesson ends with three check questions. From Lesson 2 on, one of them looks back at an earlier lesson. Each activity produces a record (a table, a graph, or a diagram) that a grown-up can look at. The final project uses a four-part checklist.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: how this unit is designed
:open:

Like Level 0, this unit was planned backward from its outcomes [@ubd_whitepaper], and each lesson moves from recall, to an example, to doing, to talking it through [@merrill2002], with built-in retrieval and spaced review [@dunlosky2013]. New in Level 1, each lesson has a short animation made with Manim Community Edition, an open-source animation library forked from the tool Grant Sanderson created for his 3Blue1Brown videos [@manim_ce]. The animations use the course's own tested simulation code, so the numbers on screen match the text. The probability ideas follow OpenStax Introductory Statistics [@openstax_prob; @openstax_design]; the wave ideas follow OpenStax Physics [@openstax_waves; @openstax_interference].

**Ways to adapt it.**

- Each story comes with an animation and a written description of what it shows.
- Learners can show what they know in a table, a graph, a drawing, or by explaining aloud.
- Each lesson has a way to make it easier and a way to make it harder.
- Activities use cheap household things, and the animations play without sound.
