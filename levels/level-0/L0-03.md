## What you will learn

- I can draw and write what I see at different times.
- I can tell what I saw apart from why I think it happened.
- I can write my why ideas in their own place.

## Story: The crying ice cube

Parsa puts an ice cube on a plate. After a while, Ryan points at it. "Look, the ice cube is sad. It is crying!"

Parsa laughs. "Ice cubes do not feel sad. Let's write only what we can see." He looks closely. He writes, "The cube is smaller. There is water on the plate."

Every five minutes, they observe the cube again. Each time, Parsa draws it and writes what he sees. The cube gets smaller and rounder. The puddle gets bigger.

Ryan has ideas about why. "Maybe the room is warm. Maybe the plate is warm." Parsa says, "Good ideas! Let's put them in a different column." Now they have two lists: what they saw, and what they think made it happen.

## Hands-on: Ice cube watch

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- One ice cube
- A plate
- A clock or timer
- Paper and a pencil

**Steps:**

1. Fold your paper in half. On the left side, write "I saw." On the right side, write "I think it is because."
2. Put the ice cube on the plate. Set the plate on a table.
3. Observe the cube right away. This is 0 minutes. Draw it and write one thing you see on the left side.
4. Set the timer for 5 minutes. When it rings, draw the cube again and write what you see now.
5. Do it again at 10 minutes and at 15 minutes.
6. Look at your drawings. What changed? What stayed the same?
7. Now think about why the ice changed. Write your ideas on the right side only.

:::{tip} Record it
A paper folded into two columns, I saw and I think it is because. Draw the cube four times, at 0, 5, 10, and 15 minutes.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
The ice will melt into water on the plate. Keep the plate away from screens, plugs, and cords, and wipe up spills so no one slips.
:::

## Practice together

Ryan says, "The ice cube got smaller because the room is warm." Which part goes in the I saw column? Which part goes in the other column?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
"The ice cube got smaller" goes in I saw, because you can see it. "Because the room is warm" is an idea about why, so it goes in the other column.
:::

## Try it yourself

**1.** You leave a piece of bread out on a plate overnight. In the morning it is hard. Write one I saw sentence.

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
Something like "The bread is hard now" or "The bread was soft before and is hard now." It tells what you saw, not why.
:::

**2.** Amin says, "The puddle dried up because the sun took the water." Is that what Amin saw, or an idea about why?

:::{dropdown} Check your answer
It is both. "The puddle dried up" is what Amin saw. "Because the sun took the water" is an idea about why, so it goes in the other column.
:::

## Talk about it

- Why did Parsa say ice cubes do not feel sad?
- Why is it useful to keep your why ideas in a different place?
- What else changes when you wait and watch? How could you observe it?

## Words to know

**observe**
: To look closely and notice what is there.

**melt**
: When something solid, like ice or butter, warms up and turns runny.

**column**
: A part of a page that goes from top to bottom.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** Telling an observation apart from an explanation is a core habit in the K-2 science practices, where children make observations to collect data and then use evidence to build explanations [@ngss_appf]. Looking at fixed times (0, 5, 10, and 15 minutes) makes before and after concrete. The ice melts because heat moves into it from the warmer air and plate. The child does not need that answer yet; confirm it after their own ideas are written down.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- The ice is sad or wants to melt. (Gently ask: what can you see? Feelings words describe people and animals, not ice.)
- The ice disappears. (The water is right there on the plate; point to the puddle.)
- A guess is only allowed if you are sure. (Guesses are welcome; they simply go in their own column until they are tested.)

**To make it easier.** Observe only at 0 and 15 minutes. The child draws, and the grown-up writes the words the child says.

**To make it harder.** Put a second ice cube on a plate in a sunny spot or near a warm window. Before you test it, ask the child to say which cube will melt first and why, and write that idea in the right column. This leads into fair tests in Lesson 4.

**Limits.** Watching a change shows what happened, not why. The ideas in the right column stay untested until a fair test in Lesson 4. How fast the ice melts depends on the size of the cube and how warm the room is, so results will differ from home to home.
