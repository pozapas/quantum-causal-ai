## Hands-on: Big ideas sort

*About 20 minutes.*

**You need:**

- A big sheet of paper
- Crayons or markers
- Sticky notes or small paper squares

**Steps:**

1. Draw three big circles that overlap, like the picture.
2. Write a name on each circle. Causes. Tiny things. Learning machines.
3. With your grown-up, write one idea on each note. Use five or six ideas.
4. Here are some ideas to use. A fair test. A clue is not a cause. Tiny things follow surprising rules. A bit holds 1 or 0. A pattern helps you guess.
5. Read each note. Which circle does it fit? Put it there.
6. If a note fits two circles, put it where they overlap.
7. Write "this course" in the middle, where all three circles meet.
8. On the back, finish this. Three things I learned in Level 0 are...

:::{tip} Record it
Your circle poster, plus three sentences about what you learned.
:::

:::{warning} Stay safe
A grown-up handles any scissors. Keep sticky notes and marker caps away from babies and toddlers.
:::

## Words to know

**learning machine**
: A computer program that finds patterns in lots of examples.

**overlap**
: When two shapes share some space.

:::{dropdown} For grown-ups: about this lesson
:open:

**Why it matters.** This lesson closes Level 0 by naming the three strands the whole course weaves together. The first is causal inference: telling a clue (an association) from a cause, and using interventions like fair tests to find out [@pearl]. The second is the world of the very small: light and the tiniest bits of matter follow rules that differ from everyday experience, and the course later meets the quantum bit, a basic unit of quantum information [@ibm]. The third is machine learning: systems that find patterns in many examples. The overlaps matter most: a learning system finds associations, and an association is not always a cause.

**Watch for these mix-ups.**

- Learning machines think or understand like people. (They find patterns in data; that is useful but different.)
- A quantum bit is just a smaller ordinary bit. (It follows different rules, met in later levels; simple everyday pictures of it can mislead.)
- The flashlight lesson showed the surprising quantum rules. (Lesson 7 was about ordinary light and seeing; the surprising rules come much later.)

**To make it easier.** Use only two circles, Causes and Learning machines, with three notes. Add the third circle another day. A placement key for the suggested notes: a fair test and a clue is not a cause go in Causes; a pattern helps you guess goes in Learning machines; a bit holds 1 or 0 goes where Tiny things and Learning machines overlap; tiny things follow surprising rules goes in Tiny things. The picture's note that measuring tiny things can change them previews a later idea; it is not shown in Level 0.

**To make it harder.** Ask the child to write one new note for each overlap and explain where it goes. Keep the notes for the start of Level 1.

**Limits.** This lesson only names the three ideas. It does not teach how quantum systems or learning machines work, and the circle sort has no single right answer for every note. Placement is a way to talk, not a test.

**On the computer.** Grown-ups can see where this idea leads later in [Lab: Confounding and intervention](../../labs/03-causal-inference.ipynb), written for older learners.

**Sources for this guide.** [@pearl; @ibm]
