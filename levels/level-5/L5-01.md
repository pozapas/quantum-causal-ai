## Learn

A qubit is a quantum system with two basis states. Write these states as \|0&gt; and \|1&gt;. A pure state is a normalized complex vector. Its amplitudes describe how the state relates to a selected measurement basis.

The Born rule converts amplitudes to probabilities. Square the magnitude of each amplitude. Add the probabilities to check normalization. A negative amplitude is permitted. A negative probability is not permitted. The measurement basis must always be stated.

A measurement gives one outcome per trial. It does not expose all amplitudes from one system. Estimate probabilities with many equally prepared systems. Use other measurement bases to learn relative phase. Measurement statistics alone do not establish a causal explanation.

:::{important} Key relation
:class: qcai-equation
\|psi&gt; = alpha\|0&gt; + beta\|1&gt;; \|alpha\|² + \|beta\|² = 1; P(1) = \|beta\|². Here alpha and beta are complex amplitudes.
:::

:::{tip} Worked example
Prepare \|psi&gt; = sqrt(3)/2 \|0&gt; + 1/2 \|1&gt;. The squared magnitudes are 3/4 and 1/4. In 400 independent computational-basis trials, the expected number of outcomes 1 is 100. An observed count need not equal its expectation.
:::

## Practice

:::{admonition} Exercise
:class: tip

A normalized state has amplitudes 1/sqrt(5) and 2i/sqrt(5). Find P(1) and the expected count in 250 trials.
:::

:::{dropdown} Solution

The squared magnitude of 2i/sqrt(5) is 4/5. Thus P(1) is 0.8. The expected count is 250 times 0.8, or 200. The imaginary phase does not change this basis probability.
:::

## Check your understanding

**What must sum to one for a normalized pure state?**

- **A.** The amplitudes
- **B.** The squared amplitude magnitudes
- **C.** The measurement counts

:::{dropdown} Show the answer
**B. The squared amplitude magnitudes**

Probabilities are squared magnitudes. Their sum is one. Complex amplitudes themselves can cancel and need not sum to one.
:::

:::{caution} Scope and assumptions
The pure-state description assumes a specified preparation. General preparations require density operators.
:::
